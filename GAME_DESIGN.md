## Summary

A first draft for brainstorming a new, independent Ren'Py game series. In it, an ordinary person learns that monsters hide inside a world forced into one language, and becomes a hunter by learning another. Everything not in the confirmed brief is a proposal.

## Craft lessons applied

- Inciting incident: it must be a real event that upends the hero's life (McKee, Story, p. 98–104). Chapter 0 opens on one.
- Character is choice under pressure (Story, p. 56). Every hunt ends on a choice, not just a win.
- Gap between expectation and result (Story, p. 85). A spell can misfire and turn the scene.
- Story type: Dude with a Problem growing into Rites of Passage (Save the Cat! Writes a Novel genre list). Fun and Games is the promise of the premise (p. 14).
- Comedy comes from 2–3 clear traits acted on consistently, plus contrast (Dikkers, ch. 3, pp. 7–8).
- Dialogue is action: each line tries to get something, and the meaning sits in subtext (McKee, Dialogue, p. 3). Upper ranks test that subtext.
- Heroes don't have to be nice, just compelling (S-O-B essay, p. 1). Dani and Marisol have sharp edges.

## Proposed world mechanism

- **The Flattening:** Three generations ago, after a catastrophe blamed on misunderstanding, the world adopted one common speech. Every other language was banned from schools, signs and broadcasts. People were promised peace.
- **Who gains:** Hidden predators, the Unworded, live in what a single language leaves unnamed. The fewer ways a society has to describe things, the more places they can hide. They quietly fund the Bureau of Common Speech.
- **The Blank:** When a monolingual mind meets an Unworded, it finds no words for what it sees, so the memory slides away like a dream.
- **Why a second language works:** A mind that actively thinks in two languages has two sets of categories. Where they don't overlap, the Unworded show up. This is a fictional rule of this world, not a claim about real languages or their speakers.
- **Symmetry:** No language is magic in itself. The power comes from bridging into any living language other than the one forced on the world. In a Spanish-interface game, the world is flattened into Spanish and English becomes the key.
- **Why not every speaker is a hunter:** Few people learn a banned language at all. Knowing words isn't enough: power needs a Waking, a first encounter you survive by deliberately speaking the second language. Most secret bilinguals have never had one, and the Blank wipes their close calls.
- **Where power comes from:** Meaning, not memorized words. Utterances work when the player understands and builds a message that fits the situation.

> **Note:** The target language is shown as heritage, music, family and resistance. Its speakers in the story are varied people with ordinary flaws. Monsters have no language at all, and the villains are the people and systems that forced one language on everyone.

## Opening encounter and chapter 0

1. **Night shift** — You clean the Records Hall, where banned books wait to be pulped. Light banter with Del sets up the comedy.
2. **The wrong voice** — Del starts answering only in phrases from the building's safety announcement. Something is wearing him like a coat.
3. **The radio** — A confiscated radio in the basement picks up Marisol's broadcast: "No le contestes. Escucha." You recognize your grandmother's lullaby in the music.
4. **The Waking** — First guided duel: repeat, then choose, a short Spanish line ("Te veo."). For a moment you see the Echo's true shape and escape with Del.
5. **The tower** — You follow the signal to the water tower. Marisol won't let you in until you introduce yourself in Spanish (an A1 task).
6. **Fun and Games** — Explore the bus depot, read signs, question a frightened driver, hunt the Echo's nest.
7. **Boss** — The Tenant, an Echo living in the city's public-address speakers. Beating it takes listening, reading and short production together.
8. **Hook** — Among the confiscated files you find your grandmother's name on a Bureau list.

Proposed length: 30–45 minutes of play.

## Proposed cast

*On the Page this section has a view switch (You / Marisol / Toby / Dani / Pryce / Del); every view is included in this export.*

### You

- **Role:** Player character, night caretaker at the city Records Hall. Name and gender chosen by the player (proposed default: Rowan Vale).
- **Wants:** A quiet, safe life and to pay off a dead parent's debts.
- **Needs:** To stop hiding from grief and from a family language nobody ever explained.
- **Flaw:** Avoids conflict by going silent, which is exactly what the monsters feed on.
- **Contradiction:** Remembers every word of a grandmother's lullaby, but has never understood a line of it.

### Marisol

- **Role:** Mentor. Retired court interpreter who runs an illegal late-night radio show from a disused water tower.
- **Wants:** To pass living languages on before the Bureau finds her.
- **Flaw:** Secretive and controlling. She trusts recordings more than people.
- **Contradiction:** Broadcasts to thousands every night but won't let anyone into the tower.
- **Comic beat:** After midnight she refuses to speak the official language on principle, even when ordering pizza.

### Toby

- **Role:** Comic relief and fellow learner. Hosts a conspiracy podcast that's right about everything for the wrong reasons.
- **Wants:** To be famous and to be believed.
- **Flaw:** Overconfident. He bluffs instead of asking.
- **Contradiction:** Fearless around monsters, terrified of verb endings.
- **Comic beat:** His wrong answers give the player errors to spot and correct, so the teaching works through him. The jokes are about learning, never about the target language or its speakers. Archetype mix: Naif and Fighter.

### Dani

- **Role:** Rival field hunter, heritage speaker and relic thief. Optional slow-burn romance (proposed for game 2 or later).
- **Wants:** Money and freedom, with no causes and no crews.
- **Flaw:** Leaves people before they can leave first.
- **Contradiction:** Mocks your grammar, yet secretly kept a childhood workbook from the banned schools.
- **Comic beat:** Lovable Scoundrel. Every rescue comes with an invoice.

### Pryce

- **Role:** Human antagonist: Director of the Bureau of Common Speech.
- **Wants:** Peace, which he believes requires one shared language.
- **Flaw:** Mistakes control for safety.
- **Contradiction:** Keeps a locked box of his mother's letters, written in a language he can't read.
- **Arc seed:** He doesn't know the predators exploit his Bureau. His turn can come in a later game.

### Del

- **Role:** Coworker who becomes the first victim. Rescued in chapter 0, he then becomes the crew's tech support.
- **Wants:** To get promoted out of the night shift.
- **Flaw:** Rule-follower. He reports everything.
- **Contradiction:** Afterwards he reports the monster to HR through official channels, every time.
- **Comic beat:** Deadpan straight man (Robot archetype) next to Toby.

Comic dynamics: Toby (bluffing learner) next to Del (literal-minded rule-follower) next to Marisol (stern purist) makes three contrasting traits for steady, low-cost humor. Dani keeps romantic tension going.

## Proposed monster threats

| Threat | What it does | Language counter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Echoes (E) | Wear victims' voices and can only repeat things already said | Listening: notice it can't make a new sentence |
| Gloss moths (E–D) | Swarms that eat the words off signs, leaving dead zones nobody can read | Reading: restore signs and labels |
| Nobodies (D–C) | Take people's names, so friends forget them | Speaking: describe and name the person to bring them back |
| Lullers (C) | Calm announcements that lead people into the harbor | Spot tone and intent; counter-announce |
| Tallymen (B) | Feed on contracts and fine print | Negotiate, register, legal reading |
| Mirrorwrights (A) | Forge documents and impersonate people perfectly | Catch the forgery through period, region and style |
| The Unsaid (S) | Lives in what a whole culture can't put into words | Irony, wordplay, mediating between people |

## Language ranks E to S

| Rank | CEFR | Example hunter task |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E | A1 | Introduce yourself, ask a ghost child's name, follow 3-step directions, read a missing-person flyer |
| D | A2 | Question a witness about last night, read a bus timetable, write a short warning note |
| C | B1 | Follow a radio news report, calm a frightened shop owner, describe how a monster behaves |
| B | B2 | Negotiate a Tallyman's contract clause, catch a lie through subtext, summarize a lecture |
| A | C1 | Interpret live under pressure, analyze archive letters, write a persuasive appeal |
| S | C2 | Decode puns and riddles, spot forgeries by dialect and era, mediate a treaty with the tone intact |

Ranks show the level each game aims for. They aren't certification. S rank means precision, nuance and mediation between people, not simply a big vocabulary or sounding native.

## Learning loop

**Diagram — nodes:**

- Explore (Read signs and notes)
- Investigate (Listen and reply)
- Hunt (Tasks under pressure)
- Debrief (Night log review)

**Connections:**

- Explore → Investigate
- Investigate → Hunt
- Hunt → Debrief
- Debrief → Explore (spaced review) [dashed]

- Every chapter covers all four skills: listening (radio, voices), reading (signs, files), speaking (building or typing lines), and mediation (helping others understand).
- Power comes from a hidden mastery model per word and grammar point (accuracy, speed, recall over time, choosing the right register), not from the number of quizzes answered.
- Help ladder: highlight known words, then a picture hint, then an English gloss (costs Focus), then a model answer and a retry.
- Errors that break meaning cause a misfire, with a story consequence and sometimes a joke. Form errors still give partial power and show the correct version.
- Input gets harder as you rank up: tile-built sentences at E, then typed answers with tolerant matching, then free answers judged by intent.

## Multilingual structure

- The story bible is language-neutral: the forced language is always the player's own, and the key is always the language they're learning.
- Interface text uses Ren'Py's translation system. Target-language content is written for each language, not machine-translated.
- Each pair gets a pedagogy module: word lists, grammar order and tasks tagged by CEFR can-do statement (e.g. kana for Japanese, cases for German).
- Each target language gets its own culture pack (songs, texts, the mentor's background), written with native-speaker reviewers.
- Shared engine: task types, the mastery model and the help ladder stay the same for every pair.

## Proof of concept scope and tests

- **Content:** Chapter 0 only: water-tower hub, Records Hall, bus depot, 4 named characters, Echoes and the Tenant boss.
- **Language:** A1 slice: greetings, ser for identity, hay, numbers 1–20, colors, tener, present-tense -ar verbs, qué/quién/dónde. About 80–100 words.
- **Not included:** Voice input, real recorded audio, romance, later ranks.

1. Testers complete 5 A1 tasks without hints at the end that they couldn't do at the start.
2. Most testers say they want to know what happens next.
3. Testers can explain why a misfire happened.
4. A quick check 48 hours later shows they kept most of the target words.
5. Humor lands, and nothing reads as mocking the language or its speakers.

## Series arc sketch

Proposed six games, one per rank. Mystery thread: who caused the Flattening? The Unworded fed on it, but frightened people chose it. The final temptation at S rank is to become the new single voice. Winning means a world of many voices, not swapping one forced language for another.
